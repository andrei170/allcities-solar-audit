AllCities Solar Power+ — Who You Are Actually Competing With

Prepared for Paul Comiskey · 30 August 2026 · AG1 Consulting
The finding, before anything else

You named four out-of-state operators who came into your market hard: Solgen, Lumio, Freedom Solar and Revolution Solar. Every one of them has failed.

Your own account on our call: Solgen went bankrupt and was pushed out of the state, paid a $250,000 fine, came back as Purelight Solar, and was gone within a year — leaving customers and banks stranded. Published reporting adds that Solgen ceased all Washington operations on 13 January 2026, listing liabilities of $50–100 million and between 25,000 and 50,000 creditors nationally, closing sites in Pasco, Spokane, Lakewood, Kent, Fife and Everett. Lumio filed Chapter 11 in September 2024, converted to Chapter 7, and sold its assets for $4 million.

And the number that matters is yours, not ours. You told us those companies left roughly 2,000 customers in your area, and that you have corrected and made whole about 15 of them.

Fifteen out of two thousand. That is not a competitor analysis. It is a list of people who own solar, have a fault, have no warranty and no company to ring — and who live in your counties.

The Four You Named
Bankrupt · WA operations closed Jan 2026

Solgen Power / Purelight Power — Pasco, WA

Started in a Pasco garage less than a decade ago and grew fast. Declared bankruptcy listing liabilities between $50 million and $100 million and between 25,000 and 50,000 creditors. All Washington operations ceased 13 January 2026, closing sites in Pasco, Spokane, Lakewood, Kent, Fife and Everett. Fifty-three jobs went in Pasco alone.

Why they went: lawsuits in several states alleging fraudulent misrepresentation, negligence and deceptive sales practices, many involving elderly customers. A separate Washington class action over unlawful non-compete clauses. And eleven Labor & Industries inspections since 2021 over fall-protection failures.

What it means for you: they sold hard, across your counties, and their customers are now stranded. That is the single biggest commercial fact in this document.

Chapter 11 → Chapter 7 · In liquidation

Lumio — national residential installer

Filed Chapter 11 on 4 September 2024 in Delaware, then converted to Chapter 7. Assets were sold to Zeo Energy for $4 million in cash and stock — against a credit bid that had been reported at $100 million.

As of early 2026 the liquidating trust was working through roughly 950 filed proofs of claim and pursuing litigation against former insiders. The claim-objection deadline was extended to 17 August 2026.

What it means for you: another national operator that sold into Washington and is no longer able to service anything it installed.

The campaign this argues for
  • Orphaned system service and repair. “Your installer went out of business. We are still here.” These people already own solar, are already unhappy, and need somebody local and licensed. No tax credit is involved, so the January change does not touch this line at all. You are already doing this work reactively when a bank or the utility calls you — this simply makes it something you win on purpose.
  • System takeover, inspection and fault-finding — the work an electrical contractor can do that a sales-led solar outfit never could.
  • Panel and inverter replacement on systems whose original supplier no longer exists.
  • Battery retrofit to existing systems — an upgrade sale to people who already believe in solar.

This is the fastest revenue in this document, because the customers already exist, they have a problem today, and there is no competition for them.

The one to worry about

Ellensburg Solar — about 35 miles away

This is the competitor doing everything you are not. They are running a Google Ads conversion tag, GA4 and call tracking — which means they know what a phone call costs them and you do not know what anything costs you.

And they are coming straight at your towns. What they do not have that you do: 47 years in the valley, an electrical contractor's licence, and wind. What they have that you do not: a form, a tracked phone number, and a page for every town.

The two reaching in from the coast

A&R Solar is a Seattle firm with Portland and Seattle phone numbers — and a page built specifically for Yakima, with a Google Ads tag behind it. They are paying to appear in your market from the other side of the mountains.

Solora Solar is also Seattle-based with a Yakima page, running a Meta pixel rather than Google Ads and using 800 numbers. Social rather than search.

Neither of them can send somebody out this afternoon. That is the argument, and neither of them can answer it — but it only works if you appear on the page beside them.

Doing badly on advertising — which is where the ground is

Solora is running social advertising with no Google Ads tag we could find, which means the high-intent search traffic — the person typing “solar company Yakima” today — is being fought over by fewer companies than you would expect.

Nobody in this group displays a rating or review count on the pages we read. Not one. In a market where two big operators just went bankrupt on the back of complaints, a visible review count is the most valuable thing on the page and it is sitting unclaimed.

Nobody is marketing to the orphaned Solgen and Lumio customers either. That is the clearest open ground in this document.

The Market Itself — Yakima And Central Washington
The single best thing happening in your market right now
Electricity in Yakima about 15 cents per kWh (Pacific Power) Pacific Power has been seeking a rate increase of around 14% What that does shortens payback on every system you quote

This is the argument that replaces the tax credit. The 30% federal credit made solar cheaper. A rising electricity price makes solar more valuable — and it does it every month, permanently, without Congress being involved.

Nobody in your market is running that campaign. The industry is still writing copy about an incentive that expired. The company that instead says “your power bill is going up again — here is what that does to the maths” is selling on something the customer can see on their own statement.

Confirm the current rate case status before it goes in any advertising, because rate filings change and get amended. But the direction of travel is the story.

The Yakima Valley is unusually good ground for commercial solar

You are sitting in one of the most agriculturally intense areas in the United States — orchards, vineyards, hop yards, packing houses, cold storage and irrigation. Those are not small electricity users. Cold storage runs year-round and irrigation pumps run hard through exactly the months your sun is strongest.

And Section 48E still applies to commercial systems through 2032. So while the residential purchase argument got harder in January, the agricultural and commercial argument did not change at all. That is the half of the market almost every residential-focused installer is not equipped to serve — and you are a licensed electrical and general contractor, which is exactly what a packing shed needs.

The buyer is different and better. A farm business owner buys on payback arithmetic rather than feelings, decides faster, signs bigger, and does not need persuading that energy is a real cost.

Sun and wind in the same place

You told us central Washington has both strong sun and strong wind, and that this is why you looked at wind in 2007. That combination is genuinely unusual and it is the whole basis of the rebrand.

Most installers in the country can sell one or the other. Being able to walk a property and honestly recommend solar, wind, storage or a combination of the three is a position no competitor on the list above can occupy — and it works best exactly where you are: rural properties, farm buildings, and off-grid sites where the grid connection is expensive or unreliable.

The caution we gave you stands. Code and permitting is what stopped wind for you in 2007 and it is county-level. Confirm the position for each county before October, because the marketing for it should not be built until the permitting is certain.

Two things that make this market harder, said plainly
  • Washington is not a high-electricity-price state overall. At around 15 cents your customers pay less than California, so payback periods are naturally longer and the emotional urgency is lower. That is why storage and outage resilience often sell better here than pure bill savings.
  • Your counties are geographically large and thinly populated compared with a metro market. Yakima, Kittitas, Klickitat, Benton and Franklin cover an enormous area. That is good for competition — there is far less of it than in Seattle or Portland — but it means drive time is a real cost, and advertising should be aimed county by county rather than sprayed across all five.

Both of these argue for the same thing: fewer, better-qualified enquiries with the county and the property type known before anyone gets in a van. Which is precisely what the enquiry form in the audit is for.

And one thing sitting outside your own front door

You mentioned you have no proper signage at the new premises on South 1st Street, and that roughly 35,000 cars a day pass it.

That is the cheapest media buy available to you and you already own the position. A sign is a one-off cost with no monthly fee, no bidding, and no platform between you and the audience — and it reinforces every other channel, because people who have seen your name on the street click your ad more readily than people who have not.

We would do this before spending anything on advertising. The traffic figure is yours and we have not verified it, but even at a fraction of that number it is the best value impression you can buy in Yakima.

The Positioning This Points To
One sentence, and it is defensible

“The companies that sold hard and left are gone. This company has been in Yakima since 1979, we are licensed electricians, and we will still be here when your system needs looking at.”

Note what that line does not say. You told us plainly you do not want to advertise being the biggest or the baddest — you want to be the best and the most memorable, a company that stood the test of time and came out progressive. Nothing above asks you to be loud. It asks that the people already looking for what you do can find you.

That line does three jobs at once: it separates you from the operators who damaged the industry's reputation locally, it speaks directly to the customers they stranded, and it makes your age — the thing you cannot change — the reason to choose you.

It is also the honest position, which matters, because Solgen's customers have already been sold to dishonestly once and will recognise it instantly if it happens again.

What We Would Run First
  • Orphaned system rescue — the stranded Solgen and Lumio customers. Fastest money, zero competition, no tax credit needed
  • Commercial and agricultural — Section 48E survives to 2032, and the Yakima Valley is full of large electricity users
  • Battery and backup — sold on outages, not on payback
  • Electrical work — the steady floor that no solar policy change can touch

Notice what is not on that list: residential solar to new customers on a cash purchase. That is the one the credit change broke, and it is the one everybody else will try to sell you. We would come back to it once the four above are running.

Your two biggest competitors went bankrupt and left you their customers.

Nobody is talking to those people. You are the licensed local firm that has been here since 1979. That is the campaign.
