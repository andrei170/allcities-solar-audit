The Constraint That Decides Everything
Starter package — what the budget actually buys

At $1,250 of spend you can run two campaigns properly or four campaigns badly. Split four ways, each gets around $300 a month and none of them ever gathers enough data to learn anything.

So the order matters far more than the list. You said on the call that you wanted commercial, small business and agricultural, that orphan systems was “real good”, and that battery backup is coming whether anybody likes it or not. All three are in here. The question is only which two go first.

Campaign One — Commercial, Small Business And Agricultural
60 to 70% of the budget. Your words: "you definitely want to target commercial, small business, agricultural."

Section 48E runs to 2032 and did not change in January. That is half the market, and it is the half where the deadline pressure hitting residential simply does not apply. You are not competing against panic and you are not discounting against a clock.

You are a licensed electrical and general contractor, which is what a packing shed actually needs. Most residential solar outfits cannot touch that work at all, and the business has held that capability in Yakima since 1979.

The Valley is agriculturally intense and these are not small electricity users. Farms, orchards, wineries, packing houses, cold storage, irrigation. Power is their largest controllable cost and the credit is still there.

Campaign Two — Battery Backup And Storage
30 to 40% of the budget. Your words: "it's going to be necessary in another three to five years."

Storage keeps the full federal credit, which makes it the strongest residential offer still standing.

But in your market the reason people buy it is not money. It is the well. No power means no water — for the house, the livestock and the stock tank. That is a fear-of-loss sale, not a savings sale, and it converts completely differently. Nobody runs a payback calculation on running water.

The same is true of medical need. Anyone on oxygen, refrigerated medication or a powered bed is not buying an energy product. They are buying certainty.

Storage is almost always sold on bill savings, which is the weakest argument available in a market where people have been burned by savings promises before. Selling it on consequence instead is open ground.

Campaign Three — Orphan Systems, And Why It Is Third Rather Than Never
You said this yourself, on both sides of it

You were clear that the opportunity is real: “Orphan systems is a good idea because there are a lot of people out there that would respond to that... the company I bought from is gone, and I don't know who to call. And so that's all real. Real good.”

And you were equally clear about why it does not work today: “It's not very profitable to go out and just do service work or clean up other people's messes. We do it, but we have to limit it to a certain amount of our time.”

Then you gave us the answer yourself: “If we had two people that were dedicated to spending 60% of their time doing warranty work, repair work, service work, that would be okay. Because that's an efficient way to do that.”

So this is not a campaign we are talking you out of. It is a campaign with a hiring decision in front of it. The moment those two people exist, this becomes one of the best assets in the business — because those homeowners already own systems, already need work doing, and already know their installer is gone.

Advertising into it before the team exists would buy you obligations rather than customers, and the service work would eat the crew you need for the jobs that actually pay.

Campaign Four — Electrical Work
The long game, and it does not need paid traffic yet

Every panel upgrade is a future solar lead, and electrical keeps crews busy between solar jobs. It is the smartest slow play in your business.

But it does not need advertising to fill a diary right now, and spending against it would take budget from the two campaigns that open genuinely new ground. It moves up the list the moment either of the first two is working.

Two of your three live competitors run Google Ads and you are not one of them. One of three has call tracking. Not one of them publishes a review rating. The business has forty-seven years of goodwill in Yakima and no rating on its website — and neither has anybody else, so the first firm here to publish one owns that ground on their own.

Solora is the interesting one after Ellensburg. They advertise on social rather than search, with 800 numbers and no Google Ads tag we could find — which means the person actually typing “solar company Yakima” into Google is being contested by Ellensburg alone.

How We Beat Ellensburg
What they have over you — and it is one thing, not many

They are buying the clicks and they are tracking everything. That is the whole advantage. It is not their work, their people, their premises or their reputation.

What they have is a form, a tracked phone number and a landing page. That can be built in days, and it is exactly what the website overhaul already covers.

What you have over them, in your own words

“Their customers call us and say, man, they just don't answer the phone over there.” Their own customers are already ringing you. That is not a marketing advantage you have to create, it is one you have to publish.

“Ellensburg Solar operates out of a storage unit building, and they prefer it that way, because they don't want people coming to see them.” You have premises in Yakima that people can walk into. In a market where the last three companies took deposits and disappeared, being findable is not a small thing.

“Their deal has always been cost.” A firm competing on price in a market full of people burned by cheap installers is standing in the worst possible position, and they do not seem to know it.

Since 1979. Licensed electrical and general contractor. Wind as well as solar. They cannot say any of those, and the wind capability nobody else in the Valley has at all.

So the positioning writes itself

The companies that sold hard and left are gone. Solgen went bankrupt twice. Lumio went under. Both were aggressive, both were cheap, and both were out of state.

“We have been here since 1979, we are licensed electricians, and we will still be here when your system needs us.” That is the whole message, and it is the one thing not a single competitor in that table can claim.

Beating Ellensburg does not require beating them at advertising. It requires matching their infrastructure — the form, the tracking, the page — and then saying the one thing they cannot.

Your Two Ideas — Both Good, Both Phase Two
Willy Wind and the live production meter

The live production meter is the better of the two and it is genuinely unusual. A real number from a real system, updating, in public. That is proof rather than a claim, and nobody in your market is doing anything like it. Every competitor asserts output. You would be publishing yours.

Willy Wind is a brand asset and it is a fair instinct. Solar advertising is relentlessly serious, and being the memorable one in a dull category compounds over years.

Both build brand, and brand needs traffic pointed at it first. At $1,250 a month the first job is finding which of the two campaigns pays. Once that is known, this is where the next money goes.

Why The Timing Matters
Three clocks, none of which we control

Wind season returns in October. That is the part of your business nobody else in the Valley can do at all, and it has a season. Campaigns need a few weeks to get out of the learning phase before they are worth judging, so starting in October is already starting late.

Ellensburg is already buying the clicks and tracking everything. Every month that stays true they take more of the orphaned Solgen and Lumio customers than you do — and they are not better than you at the work.

The residential credit position has changed. The maths a homeowner runs today is not the maths they ran last year. The first company in Yakima to explain the new position clearly and honestly wins, and that window closes the moment somebody else does it.
